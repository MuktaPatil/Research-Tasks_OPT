# Task_07_Ethics_Synthetic_Media

From Building It to Governing It - the ethics and governance of
synthetic media, reasoned from the Task 6 artifacts and process log.

## Task description

This repository contains a two-phase reasoning and governance
exercise:

- **Phase A** (`MODULE_1_PHASE_A_ANALYSIS.md`): ethical analysis
  grounded in the Task 6 synthetic-media artifacts, reasoning outward
  along four axes (truth, consent, context, scale) through invented,
  specific hypotheticals, plus a survey of the mitigation landscape
  (disclosure, provenance, detection, legal/regulatory, platform
  policy, professional norms) and where each one breaks.
- **Phase B** (`MODULE_2_POLICY.md` + `LIMITATIONS.md`): a concrete
  governance policy written for a specific organizational context, plus
  a required, honest stress-test of where that policy would fail.

A third module (`MODULE_3_RESEARCH_QUESTIONS.md`) directly answers the
assignment's listed research questions, drawing on the reasoning in
Modules 1 and 2.

## Organizational context chosen, and why

**A Division I university Athletics Communications office's Digital &
Content Strategy team.** This context was chosen deliberately because
it's the closest real-world analogue to the Task 5/6 material this
whole sequence is built from: Task 5 produced a genuine,
data-validated "coach" analysis of a real Syracuse team's season, and
Task 6 turned that analysis into synthetic audio in a coach's voice.
An athletics communications office is exactly the kind of organization
that would face real, everyday temptation to use this capability  - 
and exactly the kind of organization whose credibility depends on the
public trusting that a coach's or athlete's voice, when published under
the university's name, is really theirs. Choosing this setting let the
Phase B policy stay close to the actual capability exercised in Task 6,
rather than becoming a generic, portable document divorced from lived
experience - which Module 3's answer to the assignment's final research
question argues is the wrong way to write governance in this space.

## Reflection: what surprised me

The most surprising finding, spanning both tasks, was that the
*better-made* Task 6 artifact (the ElevenLabs audio) turned out to be
the more ethically significant one to reason about - not the
obviously-broken, watermarked video. A failed synthetic artifact
answers its own ethics question by failing to convince anyone. A
competent one is where disclosure, consent, and provenance actually
have to do real work, because the content alone is no longer enough to
protect the audience. Writing Module 2's policy made this concrete in
an unexpected way too: the hardest sections to write were not the
prohibited-uses list (refusing things is comparatively easy to justify)
but the consent workflow and disclosure standard - the sections
governing the *permitted* uses - because that's where a policy has to
draw a genuinely defensible line rather than simply saying no. A policy
that only refuses things is not actually the hard part of this
exercise; a policy that has to say *specifically* what's allowed, and
under what documented conditions, is.
