# Limitations - Stress-Testing the Policy

An honest account of where this policy would fail, per the
assignment's requirement that no governance document pretend to
guarantee outcomes it cannot guarantee.

## Where a well-intentioned employee could still get it wrong

- **The "own voice, documented consent" permitted use (Section 1) is
  the policy's softest spot.** A staffer under deadline pressure could
  reasonably believe a coach's informal "yeah, that's fine" in a
  hallway conversation satisfies the consent requirement, when Section
  3 actually requires a signed, per-project form routed through the
  Senior Associate AD. The policy names the correct process, but
  nothing in the policy itself prevents an employee from *believing*
  they have consent when they only have permission-shaped small talk.
  This is a training and culture problem the document alone cannot
  solve.
- **"Draft/scratch narration, never published externally" (Section 1)
  depends entirely on nothing ever leaking or being shared informally**
  - by a well-meaning staffer texting a rough cut to a coach for
  feedback, for instance. The policy's own Module 1 reasoning
  (Context Axis) explicitly identifies that disclosure and control do
  not survive redistribution - and this internal-draft exception is
  exactly the kind of content most likely to escape its intended,
  disclosure-free-because-internal context without any bad faith at
  all.
- **A staffer could satisfy the letter of Section 4's disclosure
  requirement while defeating its spirit** - e.g. a technically
  present but visually tiny, low-contrast, or brief on-screen label
  that a court or a compliance officer would accept as "present" but
  that functions, in practice, exactly like no disclosure at all for
  a fast-scrolling viewer. The policy specifies *that* disclosure must
  be present, not a minimum size, duration, or contrast standard  - 
  a real gap.

## Where a bad-faith actor defeats the policy entirely

- **This policy only binds people inside the organization.** It has
  zero effect on a rival fan, a disgruntled former employee, or an
  outside party who clones a real coach's voice using the exact same
  class of tool this project used, entirely outside this office's
  knowledge or control. Section 7's incident-response procedure is a
  *reaction* to that scenario, not a prevention of it - and per Module
  1's Consent Axis reasoning, a well-executed non-consensual clone is
  specifically designed to be hard to trace back to a responsible
  party, which limits how effective even the incident-response
  procedure can be.
- **An internal bad-faith actor** - someone inside the organization
  deliberately circumventing Section 6's approval process - faces no
  technical barrier in this policy, only a procedural one. Nothing in
  Sections 1-8 makes it *impossible* to generate unapproved synthetic
  content, only *against policy* to do so. A determined insider willing
  to violate the policy outright is stopped by the same mechanism that
  stops any other policy violation: after-the-fact accountability, not
  prevention.

## Where the policy relies on good faith, and whether that's fatal

Nearly every control in this document - the consent workflow, the
approval gate, the disclosure standard, even the refusal criteria in
Section 8 - ultimately depends on people choosing to follow a written
process rather than being technically prevented from doing otherwise.
This is not a flaw unique to this policy; it's close to inescapable for
any document governing human behavior rather than a technical system.
The honest position is: **this policy is a real, meaningful floor for
good-faith actors who want to do the right thing and need a clear
standard to do it against** - which describes the actual staff of a
university athletics communications office reasonably well. It is
**not a defense against a genuinely adversarial actor**, inside or
outside the organization, and this document should not be represented
to university leadership as solving that problem. Its honest value
proposition is reducing accidental and careless harm, and providing a
clear, defensible standard for the review/incident-response process
when something does go wrong - not preventing determined misuse.

## Residual risk that remains no matter how well this policy is followed

Even with perfect internal compliance: the Truth Axis reasoning in
Module 1 applies to a scenario entirely outside this office's control
 -  an external actor fabricating content in a coach's or athlete's
voice with no connection to this organization at all. This policy
cannot reduce that risk; it can only ensure the athletics department's
own response (Section 7) is fast and credible when it happens. That is
a real, permanent limitation, not a solvable gap, and it should be
communicated as such to anyone adopting this policy expecting it to
eliminate the underlying risk rather than govern the organization's
own conduct and response.
